\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Continuous emotion regression estimates moment-to-moment valence and arousal
from behavioural or physiological observations. Such estimates support
affective human--computer interaction, adaptive media, and the study of emotion
during naturalistic experience \cite{picard1997affective,russell1980circumplex}.
EEG provides millisecond-scale electrophysiological measurements, whereas
functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) captures slower haemodynamic
changes. Their complementary temporal and physiological properties make
EEG--fNIRS fusion a promising route to continuous affect modelling
\cite{bandara2018fnirs,sun2020multimodal,chen2024eegfnirs}.

A central difficulty is that affective physiology varies substantially across
people. Differences in anatomy, sensor contact, baseline state, and response
style produce cross-subject shifts that can be larger than the within-subject
emotion signal \cite{li2022eegreview,li2018crosssubject}. Existing work
therefore concentrates on invariant features, graph representations,
multimodal fusion, or subject adaptation. These approaches address
physiological variability, but they do not by themselves determine which
information source is responsible for prediction accuracy.

This attribution problem becomes acute when all participants view the same
time-aligned stimuli. Naturalistic films induce temporally reliable neural and
haemodynamic responses across viewers
\cite{hasson2004intersubject,kauppi2010isc,nummenmaa2012synchrony,
dmochowski2012correlated,dmochowski2014audience,ki2016attention}. Their affect
labels can likewise contain a shared trajectory indexed by video and time.
Holding out participants while retaining the same videos therefore tests a new
viewer on familiar stimuli, not a wholly new affective situation. Without an
explicit stimulus-only comparator, stimulus-locked group structure can be
mistaken for information decoded from individual physiology.

We address this source ambiguity rather than treating overall error as a
self-explanatory measure of physiological decoding. Our analysis separates a
training-derived video--time prior from an EEG--fNIRS predictor and rebuilds the
prior inside every subject-held-out fold. This design asks how much error is
explained by the repeated stimulus coordinates, how much remains in the
physiological branch, and whether their fusion provides measurable incremental
value. The contributions are threefold:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate source ambiguity in repeated-stimulus continuous emotion
  regression and introduce a fold-wise, source-explicit decomposition that
  prevents held-out participant labels from entering the stimulus prior.
  \item We quantify the relative contributions of shared stimulus structure and
  multimodal physiology in a 24-participant EEG--fNIRS dataset, showing that the
  former accounts for nearly all of the observed improvement over physiology
  alone under the evaluated protocol.
  \item We derive evaluation requirements for physiological claims: a
  stimulus-only baseline, crossed participant-by-stimulus splits, paired
  component residuals, and uncertainty estimates that respect participant and
  video clustering.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Physiological emotion regression}

Physiological emotion recognition commonly represents affect in categorical or
valence--arousal spaces \cite{russell1980circumplex,koelstra2012deap}. EEG work
has identified informative frequency bands and spatial patterns
\cite{zheng2015critical}, while fNIRS studies use oxygenation-related statistics
to model affective state \cite{bandara2018fnirs}. Multimodal systems combine the
two signals through graph convolution, attention, or alignment mechanisms
\cite{sun2020multimodal,chen2024eegfnirs,fang2026asac}. These methods motivate
our physiological comparator, but our main question is not whether a more
complex fusion architecture can reduce error. It is whether error reduction can
be attributed to physiology once shared stimulus structure is measured
explicitly.

Cross-subject variation remains a persistent limitation
\cite{li2022eegreview,li2018crosssubject}. Resting recordings and transfer
learning have been used to align subjects or personalise affective decoders
\cite{morioka2015resting,zheng2016personalizing}. Such adaptation is useful, but
it addresses a different source from stimulus-locked agreement. A model may
simultaneously face participant shift and benefit from knowing where a viewer is
within a familiar video; the two effects should not be conflated.

\subsection{Shared-stimulus structure and evaluation}

Naturalistic stimuli generate inter-subject synchrony in both electrical and
haemodynamic measurements
\cite{hasson2004intersubject,kauppi2010isc,dmochowski2012correlated,
dmochowski2014audience}. This synchrony is scientifically meaningful, yet it
also means that repeated-stimulus datasets contain structure shared across
participants. In continuous affect annotation, video identity and timestamp
can index a population-level response trajectory even when no physiological
sample is observed.

Evaluation must therefore match the intended generalisation unit. Grouped
cross-validation can prevent direct participant overlap, but it does not
estimate transfer to unseen stimuli when videos recur across folds
\cite{saeb2017usecase,roberts2017crossvalidation}. Model selection within the
same folds can also produce optimistic estimates
\cite{varma2006bias,varoquaux2017decoders}. Our analysis treats
subject-held-out performance as evidence for new-viewer prediction on familiar
videos and reserves unseen-stimulus claims for a crossed evaluation that the
current data record does not provide.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task and source decomposition}

For participant \(s\), video \(v\), and second \(t\), the target is
\(y_{s,v,t}\in[1,255]^2\), containing valence and arousal. The dataset comprises
24 participants, 15 videos shared by all participants, 360 trials, and 36,864
one-hertz targets; each trial also includes a five-second resting baseline
\cite{merpsdataset}. EEG contains 64 channels, and fNIRS contains six signal
types over 51 channels.

We compare three information sources on identical held-out samples:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a physiological prediction \(m_{s,v,t}\) obtained from EEG and fNIRS;
  \item a video--time prior \(\bar p_{v,t}\) constructed from training labels;
  \item a fixed convex fusion of the two.
\end{enumerate}

The prior never uses labels from the held-out participants. Consequently, the
comparison does not test leakage from those participants; it measures how much
of their affect trajectory is predictable from other viewers at the same
stimulus coordinate.

\subsection{Fold-wise video--time prior}

Within each training fold, we aggregate labels at every video--time coordinate
using the cross-participant median:
\begin{equation}
 p^d_{v,t} =
 \operatorname{median}_{s\in\mathcal S_{\mathrm{tr}}} y^d_{s,v,t},
 \qquad d\in\{V,A\}.
\end{equation}
We then smooth each trajectory with a radius-three moving average,
\begin{equation}
 \bar p^d_{v,t} =
 \frac{1}{|\mathcal I_{v,t}|}
 \sum_{u\in\mathcal I_{v,t}} p^d_{v,u},
 \quad
 \mathcal I_{v,t}=\{u:|u-t|\leq3,\ 0\leq u<T_v\}.
\end{equation}
Smoothing never crosses video boundaries. The median limits the influence of
individual trajectories, and rebuilding the prior in every fold ensures that
the labels of a held-out participant cannot determine its prediction.

\subsection{EEG--fNIRS physiological branch}

Signals are centred using the corresponding trial baseline. EEG is resampled
to 200 Hz. For each channel and one-second segment, we compute relative log
power and differential entropy in six bands from 1 to 45 Hz, together with
Hjorth activity, mobility, and complexity \cite{hjorth1970eeg}. These 15
features are concatenated over \(t-1,t,t+1\), yielding a \(64\times45\) EEG
tensor. For each fNIRS channel and each of six signal types, we compute mean,
standard deviation, slope, skewness, and kurtosis. Temporal concatenation yields
a \(51\times90\) fNIRS tensor. Edge padding is used at trial boundaries.

Each modality is encoded with a learned-adjacency, multi-order graph network
using identity, first-order, and second-order propagation supports, producing
32-dimensional channel embeddings. Four-head attention exchanges information
in both EEG-to-fNIRS and fNIRS-to-EEG directions
\cite{vaswani2017attention,tsai2019multimodal}. Mean and maximum pooling over
the modality-specific and cross-modal tokens feed a multilayer regressor.
Training minimises normalised-target mean squared error plus \(0.01\) times a
symmetric contrastive alignment loss \cite{chen2020simclr}. Models use
participant-grouped folds, AdamW optimisation, strong dropout, and early
stopping on fold validation MSE.

\subsection{Fixed fusion}

For the retained source-decomposition analysis, the fused estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \hat y_{s,v,t}
 =
 w\odot \bar p_{v,t} + (1-w)\odot m_{s,v,t},
 \qquad w=[0.99,0.92].
\end{equation}
These fixed weights strongly favour the prior. Our central evidence is therefore
the direct comparison between the prior and physiological branches; the small
fusion difference is reported as a descriptive incremental result rather than
as evidence for an optimally selected hybrid. All three outputs are evaluated
on the same held-out samples.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Subject-held-out protocol}

Participants are partitioned into five folds. For every fold, the video--time
prior is reconstructed using only training participants, and the matching
physiological checkpoint predicts the held-out participants. The full-data
checkpoint is excluded. Predictions and targets from the five folds are
concatenated before computing sample-pooled MAE for valence, arousal, and their
arithmetic mean.

This protocol isolates participants but not videos. It therefore evaluates the
specific setting of an unseen viewer responding to the same 15 stimuli. It does
not evaluate new-video transfer, and the time-aligned seconds are not treated
as independent observations for inferential claims.

\subsection{Shared stimulus structure dominates prediction}

The physiological branch obtained an overall MAE of 47.3509, with valence MAE
51.3475 and arousal MAE 43.3543. The fold-wise video--time prior reduced these
errors to 29.0633 overall, 26.6681 for valence, and 31.4585 for arousal
(Figure~\ref{fig:source-dominance}). Thus the prior improved overall MAE by
18.2876 relative to physiology alone.

Fixed fusion reached 29.0146 overall, 26.6642 for valence, and 31.3651 for
arousal. Relative to the prior, this corresponds to improvements of 0.0487
overall, 0.0039 for valence, and 0.0934 for arousal. Of the total 18.3363-point
reduction from physiology alone to fusion, 99.7\% was already achieved by the
stimulus prior. Under the evaluated repeated-video setting, shared stimulus
structure therefore accounts for nearly all of the observed predictive gain,
whereas the measured physiological increment is small.

This is a source-attribution result, not evidence that physiology is
uninformative in general. The aggregate record does not retain paired
sample-level residuals or uncertainty estimates, so the small fusion gain
cannot be localised to particular participants, videos, or time intervals and
should be interpreted descriptively.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the decomposition reveals}

The central finding is that subject-held-out emotion regression can remain
dominated by information shared across participants. Rebuilding the prior
without held-out labels rules out direct label leakage from those participants,
yet the prior still substantially outperforms the EEG--fNIRS branch. The most
plausible interpretation is that the aligned videos induce a reproducible
population-level affect trajectory that is available through video identity and
time alone.

This interpretation changes what the reported error means. Low error in a
repeated-stimulus protocol need not imply that a model has recovered an
individual's affect primarily from physiology. It may instead indicate that
the stimulus coordinate predicts the group response well. A source-explicit
prior makes this information visible and allows the physiological branch to be
judged by its incremental contribution rather than by the fused score alone.

The small increment should not be read as a general absence of affective
information in EEG or fNIRS. It may reflect the limited number of participants,
sensor noise, handcrafted one-second features, imperfect haemodynamic timing,
cross-subject shift, or an architecture that does not isolate residual
person-specific variation. The current aggregate errors cannot distinguish
among these explanations. A stronger physiological model could increase the
increment, but it would not remove the need for a stimulus-only comparator.

\subsection{Implications for emotion-regression evaluation}

Claims about physiological decoding require evaluation axes that match the
intended use. Subject-held-out, stimulus-retained evaluation supports
new-viewer prediction for familiar content. Stimulus-held-out evaluation asks
whether a model transfers affective relations to new content. Holding out both
participants and stimuli tests the most demanding form of generalisation. These
settings answer different scientific questions and should not be collapsed
into one score.

Future studies should therefore use a crossed participant-by-stimulus design
with four clearly labelled cells: familiar participant/familiar stimulus,
unseen participant/familiar stimulus, familiar participant/unseen stimulus,
and unseen participant/unseen stimulus. Every outer split should rebuild the
stimulus prior, feature standardisation, checkpoint selection, and fusion
weights using training data only \cite{varma2006bias,varoquaux2017decoders}.
Within each cell, physiology alone, stimulus prior alone, and fusion should be
evaluated on identical keys.

Reporting should also preserve the hierarchy of the data. In addition to
sample-pooled MAE, participant-macro and video-macro MAE would prevent long
trials or difficult videos from dominating silently. Paired residuals should
be retained for each participant--video--time coordinate, with uncertainty
estimated by resampling participants and videos rather than treating all
36,864 temporally adjacent seconds as independent observations.

\subsection{Limitations}

The present evidence is bounded in four ways. First, all participants viewed
the same 15 videos, so no result measures transfer to unseen stimuli. Second,
only aggregate component scores were retained; paired uncertainty and
participant- or video-specific failure patterns cannot be reconstructed.
Third, checkpoint selection and the fixed fusion weights were not evaluated
within a documented nested procedure. The absolute errors and the 0.0487
fusion increment should therefore be treated as descriptive. Fourth, the
physiological pipeline uses future one-second context and no explicit fNIRS
haemodynamic shift, making it an offline estimator rather than a causal
real-time decoder.

The dataset uses anonymous participant identifiers and does not support
demographic, clinical, or subgroup conclusions. Moreover, the graph,
cross-modal, and feature choices define only one physiological comparator.
Conclusions about source dominance are therefore specific to this dataset,
these repeated stimuli, and the evaluated modelling pipeline. The appropriate
next experiment is not another aggregate fusion score, but a crossed
participant--stimulus study with source-matched ablations and retained
sample-level residuals.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We separated stimulus-locked group structure from EEG--fNIRS evidence in
continuous emotion regression. In five-fold participant-held-out evaluation,
the video--time prior reduced MAE from 47.3509 for physiology alone to 29.0633,
while fixed fusion reached 29.0146. Shared stimulus structure thus explained
nearly all of the observed improvement under the repeated-video protocol. This
finding does not negate physiological affect information; it identifies the
limit of what subject-held-out evaluation can establish when stimuli repeat.
Source-explicit baselines and crossed participant-by-stimulus splits are
necessary to determine whether future gains arise from individual physiology,
shared stimulus dynamics, or their interaction.
